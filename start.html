Starting Your Engaged Learning Experience!

Ready to Begin?

You have found an opportunity.
Now turn that oppotunity into an experience.

Your First Conversation

Before your project starts, ask your client, faculty menber, or supervisor:

  • What problem are we trying to solve?
  • Who are the users or stakeholders?
  • What does success look like?
  • What are my responsibilities?
  • How will we communicate?
  • How often will we recieve feedback?
  • What are the important deadlines?
  • What constraints should we understand?
  • What should we do if we encounter a problem?

Your First Week

Get oriented

Meet your team and your partner

Know:

  • Your client
  • Your teammates
  • Your faculty member
  • Your supervisor
  • Your community partner
  • The people who will be affected by your work

Understand the problem

Don't rush immediately into creating a solution. First ask:

  • Who is experiencing the problem?
  • What do they need?
  • What assumptions are we making?
  • What information are we missing?

Build with, not just for

Engaged learning is collaborative.
When working with a client or community partner, remember:

Listen first

The partner understands their organization and context.

Ask questions.

Your job is not simply to receive instructions. It is to understand the problem.

Share your thinking.

Explain your design, research, or technical decisions.

Welcome feedback.

A different perspective can make the final result stronger.

Respect expertise.

Everyone involved brings knowledge to the project.

MAKE YOUR EXPERIENCE ACCESSIBLE

Accessibility Is Part of Good Information Practice.

Accessibility isn't something to consider only after a project is finished.
It should be part of the design process from the beginning.

Ask:

  • Can people with different abilities use this?
  • Can people access this information in different formats?
  • Does the interface work with assistive technologies?
  • Are videos captioned?
  • Do images have meaningful alternative text?
  • Can users navigate without a mouse?
  • Is the language understandable?
  • Are color, contrast, and typography accessible?
  • Have we included people with disabilities in our research and design process?

Accessibility Checklist

Digital

  • Keyboard-accessible navigation
  • Meaningful headings
  • Descriptive links
  • Alternative text for meaningful images
  • Captions for videos
  • Transcripts when appropriate
  • Sufficient color contrast
  • Forms with clear labels
  • Accessible PDFs and documents
  • Screen-reader compatible content

Research

  • Accessible recruitment materials
  • Accessible interview methods
  • Multiple ways to participate
  • Appropriate consent materials
  • Respect for participant preferences
  • Disability-inclusive research questions

In-Person Experiences

  • Accessible meeting location
  • Accessible entrances
  • Accessible restrooms
  • Seating options
  • Transportation considerations
  • Breaks when appropriate
  • Accessible presentation materials

REFLECT ON YOUR EXPERIENCE

What Did You Learn?

A project isn't finished when you submit the final deliverable.
Take time to think about what the experience means.

Describe

What happened?

Analyze

Why did it happen?

Connect

How does it relate to what you learned at UMSI?

Apply

How will you use what you learned next?

Reflection Prompts:

About Information

What did this experience teach you about how information works in the real world?

About People

How did working with users, clients, or communities change your understanding of the problem?

About Technology

What did you learn about the strengths and limitations of technology?

About Yourself

What did you discover about the kind of work you enjoy?

About Accessibility

How did accessibility influence your understanding of users and information systems?

About Your Future

What will you do differently in your next project?
